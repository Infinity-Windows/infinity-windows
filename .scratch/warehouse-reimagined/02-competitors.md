# Warehouse app competitor & idea research
### For a window/door installation company's conex-yard, phone-first, 1–6-piece-unit inventory problem

**Research date:** 2026-09-06
**Scope requested:** three groups — (1) general small-business/warehouse mobile inventory apps known for simplicity, (2) window/door/glass/millwork-specific software and manufacturer labeling conventions, (3) warehouse logistics UX ideas (LPN, dock receiving, visual maps, put-to-light, yard management, photo-as-location, voice, event-sourced undo, mobile scanning ergonomics).

**Context recap (so this reads standalone):** a small window/door **installer** (not a manufacturer) receives mixed truckloads from manufacturers. Each "unit" = one window/door opening on one job, identified by a mark like "#16" or "1A." A unit physically ships as 1–6 pieces (frame, glass/sash, hardware, threshold, screen) that the factory labels something like "**#16 2/3**" (piece 2 of 3 for mark #16). Warehouse staff slap an anonymous QR "license plate" on each physical piece, then stash pieces across ~8–15 rented conex containers, trailers, and one warehouse. Crews later check pieces back out to job sites. The company wants this radically simpler and more visual than a typical WMS, phone-first, and tolerant of mistakes (relabeling, mis-scans, offline).

## GROUP 1 — General small-business inventory / warehouse apps known for simple mobile UX

### Sortly
**What it does:** Visual, folder-based inventory app built around item **photos** (up to 8 per item) rather than SKU tables. Items live in a nested folder tree that "mirrors physical locations" — e.g. Warehouse → Conex 4 → Shelf B. Supports **barcode & QR code scanning** from the phone camera for lookup and updates, **custom folders**, **custom fields**, **offline mobile access with later sync**, and an **Undo Action** feature that reverses a recent move or quantity change (help.sortly.com confirms an explicit "How do I undo an action in Sortly?" flow). Bulk Edit lets you update many items/folders at once. 15,000+ businesses use it; positioning is explicitly "not a WMS, not a purchasing/fulfillment system," just visual tracking.
**Pricing:** Advanced ~$29/mo (annual), Ultra ~$89–149/mo, Premium ~$149+/mo (sources disagree on exact banding by tier name/year, but the shape — a cheap visual tier, a mid, and an enterprise-ish top — is consistent).
**What's stealable:** The **folder-tree-mirrors-physical-space** metaphor is the single most transferable idea here — a company with 8–15 conexes could model each conex as a top-level folder, each shelf/bay as a subfolder, with a photo of the *empty* bay as its icon. The **item-photo-first list view** (thumbnail grid instead of a text table) is exactly the "radically visual" ask. The **Undo Action** pattern (single-tap reversal of the last move/quantity edit, not a full audit-log dig) is a cheap, high-value UX primitive for a business specifically worried about mislabeling.
**Verdict: Adopt** (folder-as-location + photo-first list + one-tap undo).

Sources: [Sortly business inventory app](https://www.sortly.com/business-inventory-app/), [Sortly Review — theretailexec](https://theretailexec.com/tools/sortly-review/), [Sortly Undo Action](https://help.sortly.com/hc/en-us/articles/36955207860507-How-do-I-undo-an-action-in-Sortly), [Sortly Bulk Edit](https://help.sortly.com/hc/en-us/articles/360016384492-Bulk-Edit-Items-and-Folders), [Sortly pricing — FounderJar](https://www.founderjar.com/sortly-pricing/)

### inFlow Inventory (incl. inFlow Stockroom / Smart Scanner)
**What it does:** Full order/purchasing system with a **mobile app for picking and receiving**. Receiving flow: tap the yellow **Receive** button on an open purchase order, select what arrived, and it explicitly supports **partial receipt** ("if you didn't receive the entire quantity of a product, you can partially fulfill it"). Uses the phone camera as a barcode scanner (no extra hardware needed) and can batch-scan multiple items before tapping Done. Supports **GS1 barcodes**, which auto-fill lot details from a single scan. Has an optional rugged Android scanner (inFlow Smart Scanner) for teams that outgrow camera scanning.
**Pricing:** Entrepreneur ~$186/mo, Small Business ~$436/mo, Mid-Size ~$999/mo (all annualized figures per third-party trackers) — this is priced like real ERP/inventory software, not a $10/mo utility, and is likely overkill/too expensive for this company's scale.
**What's stealable:** The **receive-against-an-expected-list-with-partial-fulfillment** pattern is directly the "delivery truck drops mixed material" problem — a receiving screen pre-populated with what a PO/ASN said should arrive, letting the yard crew tick off what physically showed up (and flag shortages) beats a blank "add item" flow every time.
**Verdict: Adapt** (borrow the partial-receipt-against-expected-list UX, ignore the price tag and the ERP scope).

Sources: [inFlow mobile features](https://www.inflowinventory.com/features/mobile), [inFlow PO receiving](https://www.inflowinventory.com/support/cloud/how-do-i-create-and-complete-a-purchase-order), [inFlow GS1 barcodes](https://www.inflowinventory.com/support/cloud/gs1-barcodes), [inFlow Smart Scanner 2](https://www.inflowinventory.com/support/cloud/inflow-smart-scanner-2), [pricing — findstack](https://findstack.com/products/inflow-inventory/pricing)

### Zoho Inventory
**What it does:** Barcode/QR scanning for receiving, adjustments, and **inter-warehouse transfers** ("move items from one location to another"). Free tier caps at 50 orders/mo, 1 user, 2 locations — genuinely usable for a very small shop; Standard $29/mo, Premium $79/mo, Plus $129/mo (annualized).
**What's stealable:** Not much UX-wise beyond confirming that even budget tools treat "move between locations" as a first-class, one-tap action, not a delete-and-recreate. Useful as a pricing floor reference (a bespoke app should feel simpler *and* cheaper than this for the same job).
**Verdict: Ignore** as a product (too generic/SKU-centric for pieces-not-SKUs), but note the transfer-as-primitive idea.

Sources: [Zoho Inventory barcode scanning](https://www.zoho.com/us/inventory/kb/general-overview/barcode-scanning.html), [Zoho Inventory pricing](https://www.zoho.com/us/inventory/pricing/), [pricing summary — costbench](https://costbench.com/software/inventory-management/zoho-inventory/)

### Odoo Inventory / Barcode app
**What it does:** This is the most operationally sophisticated example found and worth reading closely. The **Barcode app** runs receiving/put-away/transfers/counts as a scan-driven flow on the warehouse floor instead of at a desk: open Operations → tap the **Receipts** card → scan each product barcode and Odoo **auto-increments the received quantity against the right line** on the expected transfer. For **put-away**, you then scan the **destination location's own label** (e.g. "WH/Stock/Bin-04") so the system records where the goods physically went, and if a **Put-Away Strategy** is configured, Odoo will *suggest* the correct destination for the operator to scan next rather than making them decide. This "system suggests the bin, human confirms by scanning the bin label" loop is the clean solution to "where do I put this piece" for a rotating cast of yard workers.
**Kits/BOM:** Odoo's Bill of Materials app supports a **Kit** BOM type — a set of components that ship/consume together under one parent product, with as many or as few components as needed, and works for variants too. This is structurally identical to "a unit is 1–6 pieces."
**What's stealable:**
1. Scan-the-bin-to-confirm-putaway (not just scan-the-item).
2. System-suggested destination that the human only has to confirm, not choose from scratch.
3. Kit/BOM as the literal data model for "unit = N pieces, each piece separately trackable, but the group is still one thing."
**Verdict: Adopt** (the kit model, the suggest-then-confirm putaway flow) — but Odoo itself is far too heavyweight/desktop-oriented to run as-is on installer phones.

Sources: [Odoo receipts/deliveries with barcodes](https://www.odoo.com/documentation/18.0/applications/inventory_and_mrp/barcode/operations/receipts_deliveries.html), [Odoo barcode scanning checklist — Kadima](https://kadimagroup.ca/barcode-scanning-in-odoo-the-warehouse-setup-checklist-that-saves-weeks/), [Odoo Kits doc](https://www.odoo.com/documentation/19.0/applications/inventory_and_mrp/manufacturing/advanced_configuration/kit_shipping.html)

### Cin7 Core / Katana
**What it does:** Cin7 Core's mobile WMS app covers "receiving, putaway, stock lookup, stocktake, transfers, picking, packing, and product-label printing," reducing "order receiving errors" via real-time sync. Katana leans manufacturing-first with barcode/QR across multiple locations and role-based mobile views. Both are $300–350+/mo entry price.
**What's stealable:** Nothing not already captured by Odoo/inFlow; these confirm receiving+putaway+transfer+label-printing is the standard feature quartet across the category, which is a useful sanity check on scope, not a source of new ideas.
**Verdict: Ignore** (same shape as the above at a higher price, no new UX pattern).

Sources: [Cin7 Core WMS](https://help.core.cin7.com/hc/en-us/articles/9034570508687-Using-the-Cin7-Core-Warehouse-Management-System-WMS), [Katana vs Cin7](https://katanamrp.com/competitors/katana-vs-cin7/)

### Fishbowl / Boxstorm (now "Fishbowl Go")
**What it does:** Fishbowl's mobile app covers barcode scanning, picking/packing/shipping, and cycle counts without dedicated scanner hardware. Boxstorm (its budget sibling) lets a phone **camera photograph a barcode and use that photo as the scan input** when a real scanner isn't handy — a nice fallback pattern for damaged/unreadable labels.
**Verdict: Ignore** as a product; note the photo-as-scanner-fallback idea as a minor detail.

Source: [Boxstorm Mobile docs](https://www.boxstorm.com/support/docs/259/)

### Ventory
**What it does:** Mobile-first warehouse app whose headline feature is **camera-based scanning that reads multiple barcodes in a single frame** ("processes multiple barcodes at once... reduces manual data entry"), plus genuinely **offline-first** operation — "stays fully functional while offline... updates synchronize automatically without data loss" once connectivity returns. Positioned for field inventory beyond four walls.
**What's stealable:** Multi-scan-in-one-frame is directly relevant: a piece often arrives as part of a cluster on a pallet, so batch-scanning several QR "license plates" in one camera pass (instead of one scan-confirm cycle per piece) would speed up bulk receiving/put-away enormously. The explicit "no data loss, syncs automatically" offline promise is a bar the company's PWA already has to clear given bad warehouse/field signal.
**Verdict: Adapt** (multi-scan-per-frame receiving).

Source: [Ventory Mobile](https://www.ventory.io/platform/ventory-mobile)

### Shelf.nu (open source) and Homebox (open source, self-hosted)
**What they do:** Both are small-team/home-scale, QR-code-first asset trackers, useful here specifically because they show what a *minimal viable* visual inventory app looks like when built by a small team, not an enterprise vendor.
- **Shelf.nu**: Every asset gets a **unique QR code**; scanning it with *any phone camera, no app required*, opens the asset's page to check in/out, update location, or view booking status. Photos attach per asset. GPS is captured automatically from the scanning device at scan time. AGPL-licensed, self-hostable via Docker, with a generous free tier (unlimited assets/QR codes/locations for one user).
- **Homebox**: Go-based, self-hosted, extremely lightweight (<50MB RAM). Generates a **QR code per location as well as per item** and ships a ready-to-print **Avery label sheet generator**. Organizes items via **Labels** (tags) and **Locations** (a tree, viewable as a tree). Has a "Bill of Materials Report."
**What's stealable:** The "**no app required — a QR scan just opens a web page**" pattern from Shelf.nu is worth strong consideration: it means a foreman's or GC's phone that has never installed the company PWA can still scan a piece's plate and see "unit #16, job Smith House, piece 2 of 3, last seen Conex 6" via a plain web link encoded in the QR — huge for the installers-in-the-field-with-bad-signal reality already described in this app's own CLAUDE.md. Homebox's **location also gets its own QR code** (not just items) is the missing half of the "photo-as-location" idea: scan the *bin's* QR to say "everything I'm about to scan next goes here," rather than scanning each piece then picking a location from a dropdown.
**Verdict: Adopt** (QR → plain URL with no login required for read-only lookup; QR-per-location, not just QR-per-item).

Sources: [Shelf.nu open source asset management](https://www.shelf.nu/solutions/open-source-asset-management), [Shelf.nu GitHub](https://github.com/shelf-nu/shelf.nu), [Homebox GitHub topics](https://github.com/topics/homebox), [Homebox project site](https://hay-kot.github.io/homebox/)

### Asset Panda / itemit
**What they do:** Enterprise-ish asset trackers (checkout/checkin, chain-of-custody, condition photos, signatures, unlimited photos per asset, GPS-at-scan). itemit specifically supports **QR + barcode + GPS + RFID simultaneously** and its check-in/out **booking system** lets staff reserve equipment ahead of time; one user quote: "Having the ability to schedule and check out equipment using QR codes has been a game changer." Both are quote-only/enterprise-priced (Asset Panda has no public pricing; itemit has a free tier + paid tiers unlocking API/RFID).
**What's stealable:** The **condition-photo-at-checkout tied permanently to the asset record** pattern (useful for damaged-in-transit disputes with the manufacturer) and itemit's **GPS-auto-logged-at-scan-time** (removes a manual "where is this" step entirely — the scan itself proves location).
**Verdict: Adapt** (auto-log GPS/location at scan time; attach condition photos to check-in/out events, not just to the item record).

Sources: [Asset Panda](https://www.assetpanda.com/), [itemit](https://www.itemit.com/)

### Tenna / Hilti ON!Track / Milwaukee ONE-KEY / DEWALT Tool Connect / ShareMyToolbox / GoCodes
**What they do (construction-tool-tracking cluster):**
- **Tenna**: GPS + BLE + QR combined; scanning a QR **starts a workflow** (find parts, attach photos/details, request the asset, submit a maintenance ticket) rather than just showing a record.
- **Hilti ON!Track**: brand-agnostic — works on *any* manufacturer's tools via rugged barcode/BLE tags, explicitly marketed as "you won't be locked in to buying... a specific brand."
- **Milwaukee ONE-KEY**: Bluetooth-only (no GPS) — a tool is only "found" when a phone running the app comes within ~300 ft; free app; also supports non-ONE-KEY tools via the "Tick" add-on tag.
- **DEWALT Tool Connect**: glue/rivet/screw-on BLE tags, IP68 waterproof, ~3-year battery.
- **ShareMyToolbox**: "#1 rated app for small tool tracking," peer-to-peer tool sharing model — tools move "from warehouse to field, or from person to person" — free tier tracks up to 100 tools, paid tier adds **GPS-on-scan** and unlimited items.
- **GoCodes**: rugged QR labels + optional GPS trackers, **no app required to scan** (any phone camera works), explicitly offline-capable labels; vendor claims 25–40% reduction in missing-equipment losses in year one; $5K–$40K/year enterprise pricing.
**What's stealable:** ShareMyToolbox's **peer-to-peer "it moves with the person, not just the shelf"** model maps well onto crews checking pieces out to a truck or a job site rather than a fixed bin. GoCodes/Hilti's "**works regardless of brand, any phone can scan, no app install**" stance reinforces the Shelf.nu QR-to-URL idea above. Tenna's "**scan starts a contextual action**, not a static lookup" is the strongest single UX idea in this cluster (see Group 3's "one scan does the right thing").
**Verdict:** ShareMyToolbox — **Adapt** (peer/person-as-a-location). Tenna/GoCodes — **Adapt** (contextual, app-free scanning). Milwaukee/DEWALT BLE tags — **Ignore** (wrong tech: proximity-only BLE tagging of individual $5 hardware pieces doesn't scale to disposable-QR-per-piece economics, and this company doesn't need indoor-proximity finding, it needs "which conex").

Sources: [Tenna QR code asset tracking](https://www.tenna.com/construction-technology/qr-code-asset-tracking-software/), [Hilti ON!Track how it works](https://www.hilti.group/content/hilti/CP/XX/en/services/tool-services/on-track/how-it-works.html), [Milwaukee ONE-KEY explainer — airpinpoint](https://airpinpoint.com/blog/milwaukee-tool-tracker-one-key), [DEWALT/Milwaukee comparison — toolguyd](https://toolguyd.com/dewalt-tool-connect-milwaukee-one-key-tracking-homeowners-2024/), [ShareMyToolbox how it works](https://www.sharemytoolbox.com/tool-inventory-app/how-it-works/), [GoCodes construction](https://gocodes.com/industry/construction-asset-tracking/)

### Kojo / StructShare (now Trimble Materials) / Procore / Buildertrend materials
**What they do:** Construction *materials procurement* platforms, adjacent but not identical to this problem (they're strong on office↔field ordering, weaker on physical bin-level put-away).
- **Kojo**: field app lets a crew **build a "shopping cart" from a BOM or project material list** and submit it to purchasing in a few taps; crucially, it lets the field **"log proof of delivery with a quick photo"** and **"log delivery notes from your mobile device,"** with deliveries auto-synced to the right PO.
- **StructShare/Trimble Materials**: consolidates requisitions, POs, RFQs, "materials receiving," multi-warehouse inventory, and invoice reconciliation into one platform; acquired by Trimble May 2025, now aimed specifically at MEP contractors.
- **Procore**: material receipt logging lets a jobsite worker "verify delivery quantities," record hauler/tracking-number/contents/comments, and "upload pictures of the items delivered" — syncs PO lines to receipts and flags delay risk automatically.
- **Buildertrend**: template-based POs tied to project phases; weaker on procurement, better on the overall build schedule.
**What's stealable:** Kojo's **photo-as-proof-of-delivery, tied directly to the PO line** and Procore's **receipt log capturing hauler/tracking#/contents/photos in one screen** are both close analogues to "the manufacturer's truck just dropped mixed material" — a receiving screen that captures one photo of the whole pallet plus a list of what showed up, rather than a photo per SKU, is the fast version of this.
**Verdict: Adapt** (single delivery-event record with one photo + hauler info + free-text discrepancy note, separate from the piece-level scan-in that follows).

Sources: [Kojo field solutions](https://www.usekojo.com/solutions/field), [StructShare/Trimble Materials](https://www.trimble.com/en/products/trimble-materials), [Procore materials](https://www.procore.com/materials), [Procore delivery log FAQ](https://support.procore.com/faq/how-do-i-track-the-actual-materials-received-and-installed-on-a-job-site), [Buildertrend comparison — ustechautomations](https://ustechautomations.com/resources/blog/construction-material-procurement-automation-comparison-2026)

## GROUP 2 — Window/door/glass/millwork-specific software and manufacturer conventions

### FeneTech FeneVision (now part of Cyncly)
**What it does:** The dominant ERP in window/door/glass manufacturing. Notably, it **eliminates manually created shipping manifests** by scanning items through production to the shipping dock, so the manifest is generated from what was actually scanned, not typed by hand. It ships a companion **FeneTech Delivery mobile app** for truck drivers — proof-of-delivery with **optional signature capture** (so a driver doesn't have to hand the phone to the customer to sign), a route-refresh button, and a distinction between "Delivered" vs. "Manually Delivered" states.
**What's stealable:** This is strong validating evidence that the manufacturer side already produces a **scan-verified shipping manifest** — meaning that, in principle, a company receiving from a FeneVision-run manufacturer could in theory get a real ASN (list of what's on the truck) rather than reconciling from a paper packing slip. Worth an actual conversation with the manufacturer's dispatch team about whether such data (even a PDF manifest) already exists and could be the seed of the company's "expected list" for receiving.
**Verdict: Adapt** (push upstream: ask manufacturers for the manifest/ASN they already generate; don't rebuild receipt reconciliation from scratch if a scan-verified list already exists somewhere).

Sources: [FeneVision ERP overview](https://fenetech.com/industries/windows-doors/), [FeneTech Delivery — Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.Fenetech.Delivery&hl=en_US), [FeneTech Delivery — App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/fenetech-delivery/id1473954764)

### A+W Cantor / Clarity, Soft Tech V6, WinDoor Quote, PMC Software (MATE), GED WinTrax
**What they do:** All are manufacturer/fabricator-side ERP+CPQ suites (quote → order → production → shipping → install/service), not warehouse-receiving tools for a downstream installer. Two details worth flagging:
- **A+W Cantor Smart Companion** is described as "mobile, real-time stock and material tracking integrated with IoT platforms" — a modern mobile layer bolted onto a legacy ERP, evidence that even fabrication-side vendors are racing toward phone-first stock visibility.
- **GED WinTrax** explicitly uses **RFID** ("intelligent logistics system tracks window assemblies through the production process to shipment") rather than QR/barcode — the only RFID-in-fenestration signal found in this research. RFID reads through packaging and doesn't need line-of-sight, but costs meaningfully more per tag and needs a reader, not just a phone camera — likely not worth it at this company's volume, but worth knowing it exists as an option if per-piece QR stickers ever become the bottleneck.
- **PMC Software's MATE** and **WinDoor Quote** are squarely quoting/production-scheduling tools (CNC file generation, DocuSign-integrated field quoting) with no receiving/logistics angle found.
**What's stealable:** Nothing directly UI-wise (these are all internal fabricator tools this company will never touch), but the RFID note is useful context, and it further confirms — see below — that **no fenestration vendor researched puts a QR code on the unit from the factory** for downstream tracking purposes; the labels found are all barcode/plain-text warranty labels, not QR.
**Verdict: Ignore** as products; **note** the RFID data point and the QR-code absence finding.

Sources: [A+W Cantor](https://www.a-w.com/aw-cantor/), [A+W Smart Companion](https://news.a-w.com/en-us/en-us/scan-better-with-aw-smart-companion-window-door-fabricators), [Soft Tech V6](https://www.cyncly.com/products/soft-tech-v6), [WinDoor Quote](https://www.windoorquote.com/window-and-door-manufacturing-software/), [PMC Software](https://pmcsoftware.com/), [GED WinTrax](https://gedusa.com/software-process-solutions/wintrax/)

### Manufacturer delivery/order-tracking programs: Andersen, Pella, Marvin, Milgard/JELD-WEN/Sierra Pacific
**What they do:**
- **Andersen**: consumer-facing "Order History Lookup" on the parts site; no evidence of a distinct "Delivery Tracker" product for installers — tracking is essentially an account-gated order-history page.
- **Pella**: **PellaPro app** gives contractors "real-time tracking updates from production to shipment"; behind the scenes Pella runs on **Cilio's Partners Portal / Customer Installations Online (CiO)**, a third-party dealer-order-automation platform, not a bespoke Pella system.
- **Marvin OMS**: a genuine order-management system for dealers with **inventory visibility** (available/allocated/backordered stock by location), **fulfillment orchestration** (pick/pack/ship/split-shipment status), and configurable **workflow automation** (routing, approvals, holds). This is the most feature-complete manufacturer-side OMS found, but it is a Windows desktop ClickOnce app (`clickonce.marvin.com`), not mobile.
- **Milgard/JELD-WEN/Sierra Pacific**: order identification is via a manually-looked-up **Sales Order (SO) number**; no dedicated tracking app surfaced in this research — dealers appear to phone/email customer service.
**What's stealable:** Confirms the industry norm is "**call/portal-lookup a numeric order ID**," not a live shipment feed — so this company should not assume better upstream data is coming; building its own receiving/tracking layer is the right call, not a stopgap.
**Verdict: Ignore** as products; **useful negative finding** (no consumer-grade ASN/API exists to integrate against today).

Sources: [PellaPro](https://pellapro.pella.com/), [Pella automate order distribution](https://www.windowanddoor.com/news/pella-implements-software-automate-order-distribution-management), [Marvin OMS overview](https://support.marvin.com/s/article/What-is-Marvin-OMS), [Andersen order tracking](https://helpcenter.andersenwindows.com/aw/s/article/How-Do-I-Track-an-Andersen-Order), [Anlin order number lookup](https://anlin.com/locate-order-number/), [Vinylmax serial number FAQ](https://www.vinylmax.com/faq-items/where-is-the-serial-number-on-my-window/)

### How manufacturers actually label multi-piece units (the closest match to "#16 2/3")
**Findings:** **Viwinco**'s published label diagrams describe a **warranty label** the company itself calls "basically the product's license plate" (their words, independently converging on the same term this company already uses) carrying order number, manufacture date, product line/style, dimensions, and distributor info; a separate **screen label** with order number + line number; and a **shipping label** carrying destination, customer name, product type, and **"order number with unit sequence"** plus route/ship date. This "unit sequence" field is exactly the "2/3" convention described in the prompt. **Cornerstone Building Brands** (American Craftsman, Atrium, Cascade, Great Lakes, Ply Gem, Prime, RELIABILT, Silver Line, Simonton, Thermo Tech — i.e., most budget/mid vinyl window brands) confirms unique per-unit product labels are applied at manufacture but does not publish their exact field layout. GS1's own logistics standard has a formal field for exactly this concept — the **ITIP (AI 8006)** identifier, "the GTIN, the piece number (2 digits), and the total number of pieces (2 digits)" for a trade item split into multiple physical pieces — which is the standards-body version of "#16 2/3."
**Evidence on QR codes from the factory:** **No evidence was found** of any window/door manufacturer researched (Andersen, Pella, Marvin, Milgard, JELD-WEN, Cascade, Sierra Pacific, Anlin, Vinylmax, Viwinco, Cornerstone brands) putting a **QR code** on the unit or carton for downstream tracking. Every label described is barcode/plain-text (order #, line #, unit sequence) plus NFRC/AAMA/Energy Star compliance labels. This is a meaningful negative finding: **the QR "license plate" this company already applies in the warehouse is doing work the factory does not do for them** — it is not duplicating anything, and there's no vendor QR standard to align with or wait for.
**What's stealable:** Two things. First, standardize the company's own internal piece-numbering display to literally mirror the "N of M" convention crews already see on manufacturer tags (their CLAUDE.md's `displayMarkCode` "1-1" style dialect could gain an explicit "piece 2 of 3" sub-field using the same vocabulary as the factory sticker, reducing translation error). Second, GS1's ITIP model — one parent identifier (the GTIN/mark) plus a piece-index/piece-count pair — is a validated, off-the-shelf data shape for exactly the "unit = N pieces" problem and could inform the schema (mark_id, piece_index, piece_count) rather than inventing one from scratch.
**Verdict: Adapt** (mirror manufacturer's "N of M" language in-app; borrow the ITIP piece_index/piece_count shape for the data model).

Sources: [Viwinco label diagrams](https://www.viwinco.com/label-diagrams/), [Cornerstone Building Brands product labels](https://www.cornerstonebuildingbrands.com/customer-solutions/windows-doors/product-labels), [GS1 Logistic Label Guideline — ITIP](https://www.gs1.org/standards/gs1-logistic-label-guideline/1-3)

## GROUP 3 — Warehouse logistics ideas and UX research

### License Plate Numbering (LPN) — the concept this company has already half-invented
**Findings:** An LPN is "a unique alphanumeric identifier applied to an individual handling unit (pallet, case, tote, carton, or roll cage) so that unit can be tracked and managed as a single entity." Crucially, "an LPN **decouples physical handling units from product identifiers**, enabling operations to move, store, pick, and ship whole containers **without repeatedly scanning every inner item**." Best practice explicitly stated: "**any product — whether one unit or multiple units — that moves together always gets an LPN, even if it is just one product**." LPNs are normally assigned **at receiving time**, either pre-printed or generated on the spot.
GS1's real-world version of this, the **SSCC (Serial Shipping Container Code)**, is literally called "the license plate of logistics" in industry writing — an 18-digit ID (extension digit + company prefix + serial reference + check digit) that lets a whole pallet or case move and be received as one scan.
**What's stealable:** This *is* the company's existing "anonymous QR license plate per piece" idea, just with formal vocabulary and one added best practice worth adopting explicitly: **decoupling** — the LPN should be able to represent a *group* of pieces moving together (a whole pallet dropped by the truck, or "everything on this dolly for job Smith House") just as easily as a single piece, so a yard worker isn't forced to scan 6 individual QR codes when 6 pieces for one mark arrive and move together; scan the pallet-level plate once, then optionally "expand" it into per-piece plates only when the pieces will later split apart (e.g., frame goes to one conex, glass to another).
**Verdict: Adopt** (support a group-level LPN that can later be "split" into piece-level LPNs, rather than only ever supporting one QR per physical piece).

Sources: [Inventoryops.com — License plates in the warehouse](https://www.inventoryops.com/articles/license-plates-in-the-warehouse.html), [Racklify LPN guide](https://racklify.com/encyclopedia/lpn-comprehensive-guide-to-license-plate-numbers-in-warehousing/), [RFgen license plating](https://www.rfgen.com/solutions/license-plating/), [GS1 SSCC](https://www.gs1.org/standards/id-keys/sscc), [GS1 SSCC as "license plate" — FLEX Logistique](https://flexlogistique.fr/what-is-sscc-serial-shipping-container-code-the-license-plate-of-logistics/)

### Dock receiving / "tailgate check" UX
**Findings:** Standard receiving process: driver checks in → gets assigned a dock/drop spot → clerk does a **quick visual inspection of trailer condition/seal** before touching product → then the receiver **validates BOL/PO/packing list/ASN against the actual shipment**, records **overages, shortages, and damages (OS&D)**, and **flags mismatches before line-item receiving even begins**. Best-practice mobile receiving checklists capture **photos, discrepancy notes, and responsible-person sign-off** so the record is audit/claim-ready. On scanner UX specifically: apps should **auto-toggle the camera scanner off** whenever a dialog, confirm box, or picklist is open, and re-enable it only when a scan is actually the expected next action — this avoids accidental captures and battery drain.
**What's stealable:** A two-stage receiving flow maps well onto this company: **Stage 1 — the "tailgate check"**: one photo of the whole truck/pallet, a big-button OK/damage toggle, and a free-text note, done in under 15 seconds, before anyone touches a piece. **Stage 2 — piece-level put-away**: only after Stage 1 is closed, scan/print/apply plates and assign conex locations. This separates "did the truck bring what we expected, in good shape" (a fast yes/no/flag decision) from "where does each piece live now" (the slower, per-piece task), which matches how installers described the two different moments of stress in receiving.
**Verdict: Adopt** (two-stage receiving: fast whole-shipment triage, then per-piece put-away).

Sources: [DataDocks — warehouse receiving best practices](https://datadocks.com/posts/warehouse-receiving-process), [GoAudits — warehouse receiving](https://goaudits.com/blog/warehouse-receiving/), [Medium — 7 UX best practices for warehouse mobile apps](https://medium.com/@stefan.karabin/7-ux-design-best-practices-for-warehouse-mobile-apps-b6e2a0a6940f)

### Visual warehouse maps ("you are here / it is there")
**Findings:** SKUSavvy's warehouse mapping product lets a user "move the warehouse map around and view individual bins as well as filled vs. empty bins to make quick decisions," offers **3D heatmaps** of movement activity, and an **"interactive inventory map search"** so staff click a found item and see exactly where it sits rather than walking aisles. CyberStockroom (industry blog, not a product) makes the underlying case well: rack-level (bin-level) mapping "turns a complex warehouse into an intuitive 2D digital twin of the space," and separately covers **laydown-yard staging**, describing turning a yard into "a living, interactive map" with **custom interactive yard maps** as a **2D digital twin**. Odoo also ships a **Smart Warehouse Visualizer** module rendering the floor plan as an interactive scene.
**What's stealable:** For 8–15 conexes/trailers plus one warehouse, a **full 2D floor-plan renderer is overkill** — but the core idea (tap a piece → see a simple visual "you are here" indicator, e.g. a photo or icon of Conex 6 highlighted on a simple yard diagram, rather than a text string "Conex 6") is exactly the "radically more visual" ask. This is cheap to build (a static yard-layout image with tappable/highlightable hotspots for each conex) and doesn't need real 2D CAD or heatmaps.
**Verdict: Adapt** (a simple static yard-map graphic with a highlighted conex, not a full digital-twin floor plan).

Sources: [SKUSavvy warehouse mapping](https://www.skusavvy.com/warehouse-mapping-software), [CyberStockroom — rack-level visibility](https://blog.cyberstockroom.com/2026/06/04/rack-level-visibility-why-mapping-down-to-the-bin-changes-everything/), [CyberStockroom — laydown yard setup](https://blog.cyberstockroom.com/2026/07/16/how-to-set-up-a-laydown-yard-for-efficient-material-staging-on-large-job-sites/), [Odoo Smart Warehouse Visualizer](https://apps.odoo.com/apps/modules/18.0/warehouse_visualizer)

### Put-to-light / color coding for low-literacy, multilingual crews
**Findings:** Put-to-light hardware uses **LED indicators + a quantity display + a confirm button per bin location**; because "instructions are visual and require no headset, no language configuration, and no spoken confirmation," it is explicitly cited as working "equally well across multilingual teams." The underlying insight — **color and light communicate a required action without requiring literacy in any language** — is the transferable piece; the physical LED hardware itself is not something a phone-first company would install in rented conexes.
**What's stealable:** Apply the *color* half of this idea in software instead of hardware: use a consistent **color badge system** per unit/piece status (e.g., red = expected but not yet received, yellow = received but not yet put away, green = put away and located, blue = checked out to a job) that reads correctly with zero text, matching the pattern this company's own codebase already uses for glow states on the fit-view map (`glowFor`: red/yellow/green/none) — meaning this is validation to extend an existing internal pattern into the warehouse module, not a new concept to introduce.
**Verdict: Adopt** (extend the existing red/yellow/green glow vocabulary from Maps Interactive into warehouse piece-status, since the crew is presumably already trained on those colors).

Sources: [Apex Warehouse Systems — pick/put-to-light](https://www.apexwarehousesystems.com/supply-chain-technologies/pick-to-light-put-to-light-systems/), [Savoye — pick/put to light](https://www.savoye.com/en-me/ressource/picking-tools-2/)

### Yard/container-yard management systems
**Findings:** Purpose-built **yard management systems** (Yard Spot, C3 Yard, RedBeam RFID, Litum RTLS) exist for trailer/container yards at a scale (thousands of moves/day, detention-fee tracking, RTLS/RFID gate reads) far beyond this company's 8–15-conex footprint. The one directly transferable idea, again from CyberStockroom's laydown-yard guide, is treating the **yard itself as a small number of named, mapped zones** with **assigned storage locations per delivery**, tracked "automatically using barcoding, GPS, RFID, and ultra-wideband" in the enterprise tier — but at this company's scale, a manually-maintained small list of 8–15 named containers (which the CLAUDE.md's warehouse-model memory already confirms is the real shape: "~8-15 rented conex containers, trailers, trucks or a main warehouse") is the right-sized version of a "yard," not a full YMS.
**Verdict: Ignore** the enterprise YMS products themselves (wrong scale entirely); **note** the "name and map every zone, even if there are only a dozen" principle as validation of the existing plan.

Sources: [Yard Spot](https://www.yardspot.io/), [CyberStockroom laydown yard guide](https://blog.cyberstockroom.com/2026/07/16/how-to-set-up-a-laydown-yard-for-efficient-material-staging-on-large-job-sites/), [GetApp yard management category](https://www.getapp.com/transportation-logistics-software/yard-management/f/location-tracking/)

### Photo-as-location
**Findings:** Multiple small consumer apps (CaptureMyItems, "Photo Inventory, Barcode GPS," BoxAeye, Sklad, Everspruce) converge on the same pattern: **every photo is bound to a specific item plus its location, quantity, and change history** at the moment of capture, and several auto-tag **GPS at the moment of the photo**. Sklad's framing is the clearest: "you never have to guess which item a photo belongs to."
**What's stealable:** For a yard worker putting a piece away, "take one photo of the piece sitting in its new spot" (rather than typing/selecting a location from a list) is faster, requires no literacy, and doubles as proof-of-condition if a dispute arises later. Combined with GPS-at-capture (already common in these consumer apps), a single photo action could both **set the location** (nearest known conex, inferred from GPS) **and record condition** in one gesture — collapsing two steps most competitors treat as separate into one.
**Verdict: Adopt** (one photo action = set location + record condition, GPS-assisted).

Sources: [CaptureMyItems](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/capturemyitems/id6762094782?uo=2), [Photo Inventory Barcode GPS](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.weserveai.photoinventory&hl=en_US), [Sklad photo features](https://sklad.app/en/features/inventory-app-with-photos)

### Voice-first capture
**Findings:** Traditional warehouse "voice picking" uses a dedicated headset, spoken instructions, and voice confirmation, integrated with a full WMS — this is mature technology but built for **dedicated indoor pickers wearing headsets all shift**, not for installers moving between trucks, conexes, and job sites with only a phone.
**What's stealable:** Very little as-is; the headset-based model doesn't fit a mobile-phone, outdoor, intermittent-use context. The transferable fragment is narrower: **voice-to-text as an alternative input method for the free-text discrepancy/condition note** (e.g., in Stage 1 tailgate-check triage above), since typing on a phone in a cold conex with gloves on is worse than talking. This is a minor accessibility feature, not a core workflow redesign.
**Verdict: Ignore** the voice-picking paradigm; **minor Adapt** (voice-to-text for notes only, using standard phone OS dictation, not a custom voice system).

Source: [RFgen voice picking](https://www.rfgen.com/voice-picking-2/), [aiola voice picking glossary](https://aiola.ai/glossary/voice-picking/)

### "One scan does the right thing" — contextual scanning
**Findings:** Tenna's model is the clearest real-world example: **scanning a QR code launches a workflow** (find parts, attach photos, request the asset, submit a maintenance ticket) rather than opening a static detail page the user then has to act on. Warehouse-app UX guidance separately recommends that **scanning itself can trigger the next state transition automatically** — e.g., "scanning the picking cart can automatically initiate a new picking process" — removing an extra tap. Scandit's UX research (an actual scanning-SDK vendor, closest thing to a primary source here) stresses that scan **feedback must be unmissable** — visual + sound + haptic together, because warehouses are loud and workers are often not looking at the screen — and that even small timing details matter: they found a **150ms delay** between the scan-confirmed beep and the camera view closing was the difference between a scan feeling "too fast to trust" and feeling reliable. They also stress **large, ergonomic tap targets** for "starting the camera hundreds of times a day," and that scanning-app design should be studied via **observation in the actual work setting**, not assumed at a desk.
**What's stealable:** This is the single most important pattern across all three groups for this specific app. A scan of a piece's QR code should be **context-aware**: scanning a piece that's "expected but not received" should default to a one-tap "mark received" action; scanning a piece that's "received, awaiting put-away" while standing in front of a conex's own QR should default to "put away here"; scanning a piece already "in stock" during a checkout flow should default to "check out to this job." The user should almost never have to navigate a menu after a scan — the system should infer the one most likely next action from the piece's current state and the last thing scanned, and just ask for a one-tap confirm (echoing the Odoo "suggest, don't ask" put-away pattern from Group 1).
**Verdict: Adopt — highest priority.** (contextual, state-aware scan actions; multi-sensory scan feedback with a deliberate confirm delay; oversized scan-trigger touch target.)

Sources: [Tenna QR code contextual scanning](https://www.tenna.com/construction-technology/qr-code-asset-tracking-software/), [Medium — 7 UX best practices for warehouse mobile apps](https://medium.com/@stefan.karabin/7-ux-design-best-practices-for-warehouse-mobile-apps-b6e2a0a6940f), [Scandit — scanning at scale UX insights](https://www.scandit.com/blog/scanning-at-scale-ux-insights/)

### Undo / "time travel" patterns — event-sourced ledgers vs. silent edits
**Findings:** The core principle from event-sourcing/ledger design: "**stock quantities are never edited directly. Every change to inventory creates a permanent movement record** that captures the item, location, quantity delta, movement type, a reference to the source event, the user who performed the action, and a precise timestamp." Corrections are handled as "**reversing and replacement entries inside the ledger itself**" — i.e., fixing a mistake **appends a new event that cancels the old one**, rather than mutating the original record — so "both the error and its resolution remain immutable, ordered facts in the log." This is contrasted with legacy CRUD systems where "the underlying posted record can still be modified," which is exactly the failure mode this company is worried about (a mislabeled piece silently overwritten with no trace of the mistake).
**What's stealable:** This validates Sortly's simpler "Undo Action" as the *user-facing* affordance while recommending the *storage* model be append-only underneath it — i.e., "undo" and "edit" in the UI should both be implemented as **new correcting events**, not destructive updates, so a foreman can always answer "who moved piece #16-2 and when, and was it ever mislabeled" months later. This directly supports the "editing a mislabeled item after the fact" requirement from Group 1's evaluation criteria and is a good fit for an app that already treats Supabase/Postgres as its system of truth (per this codebase's own CLAUDE.md) — an append-only `piece_events` table underneath a friendly "move / relabel / undo" UI is a natural, low-risk addition.
**Verdict: Adopt** (append-only event log underneath a simple undo/relabel UI, not destructive updates).

Sources: [InventoryPath — event-sourced inventory](https://www.inventorypath.com/event-sourced-inventory-why-crud-based-stock-ledgers-eventually-corrupt-and-how-to-build-an-append-only-alternative), [Formance — immutable ledgers](https://www.formance.com/blog/financial-operations/immutable-ledgers-append-only-data-models)

### Mobile scanning ergonomics (Nielsen Norman Group / Scandit)
**Findings:** NN/g's guidance on touch targets: interactive elements should be **at least 1cm × 1cm (0.4in × 0.4in)** as an absolute floor to "support adequate selection time and prevent fat-finger errors," with **~2mm minimum spacing** between adjacent targets to prevent accidental taps, and larger-than-minimum targets recommended for primary actions and for users under movement or with reduced dexterity — both of which describe an installer standing in a moving truck bed or wearing work gloves. Scandit (see above) adds the scanning-specific layer: big scan-trigger buttons, multi-sensory feedback, and deliberate timing so users trust the scan actually registered. No single primary source was found that bundles glove-specific sizing, sun-glare contrast, or dark-mode specifically for scanning apps — those appear to be practitioner folklore repeated across blog posts rather than documented NN/g or Baymard findings, so they should be treated as **plausible but unverified** rather than cited as established research.
**What's stealable:** Set a **hard floor of 1cm/44-48px touch targets with 2mm+ gaps** for every scan/confirm/undo button in the warehouse module specifically (not just relying on generic component defaults), since this is the one area of the app where fat fingers, gloves, and one-handed phone use while holding a piece are the norm rather than the exception.
**Verdict: Adopt** (explicit oversized touch targets for the warehouse module, sourced to NN/g's documented minimum rather than assumed).

Sources: [NN/g — Touch Target Size](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/touch-target-size/), [Scandit — scanning at scale UX insights](https://www.scandit.com/blog/scanning-at-scale-ux-insights/)

## Top 15 ideas ranked by impact for a 1–6-piece-unit, conex-yard, phone-first workflow

1. **Contextual, state-aware scanning ("one scan does the right thing").** Scanning a piece's QR infers the next action (receive / put-away / check-out) from its current state instead of opening a menu. *(Tenna, Odoo, Scandit)*
2. **Group-level LPN that can be "split" into piece-level LPNs.** Scan one plate for a whole pallet/dolly of pieces moving together; only generate individual piece plates when pieces are about to separate. *(LPN best practice, GS1 SSCC)*
3. **Two-stage receiving: fast whole-truck triage, then per-piece put-away.** One photo + OK/damage toggle for the truck, separate from the slower piece-by-piece scan-in. *(Dock receiving / tailgate-check best practice)*
4. **Append-only event log under a simple "undo/relabel" UI.** Every move, receipt, and correction is a new event, never a silent overwrite — the technical backbone that makes "editing a mislabeled item" safe. *(Event-sourced ledger pattern)*
5. **QR → plain web page, no app/login required for read-only lookup.** A GC or unfamiliar crew member's phone can scan a piece and instantly see what/where/whose it is. *(Shelf.nu, GoCodes, Hilti ON!Track)*
6. **One photo action = set location + record condition (GPS-assisted).** Collapse "where did I put it" and "what shape is it in" into a single gesture. *(Photo-as-location consumer apps, itemit's GPS-at-scan)*
7. **QR-per-location, not just QR-per-item.** Each conex/bay gets its own scannable code; scanning it before a batch of piece-scans means "everything next goes here." *(Homebox)*
8. **System suggests the destination; human just confirms by scanning it.** Mirrors Odoo's put-away-strategy suggestion — reduces decision-making for rotating/inexperienced yard staff. *(Odoo Barcode app)*
9. **Multi-scan-per-camera-frame for batch receiving.** Scan several pieces off one pallet in a single camera pass instead of one scan-confirm cycle per piece. *(Ventory)*
10. **Extend the existing red/yellow/green glow vocabulary from Maps Interactive into warehouse piece status.** Reuse a pattern crews are presumably already trained on rather than inventing a new color language. *(Put-to-light principle + this codebase's own `glowFor`)*
11. **Oversized, glove-friendly scan/confirm/undo buttons with multi-sensory feedback and a deliberate post-scan delay.** A documented 1cm/44px floor plus sound+haptic+visual confirmation, tuned so a scan feels trustworthy. *(NN/g, Scandit)*
12. **Single delivery-event record: one photo + hauler info + free-text discrepancy note, separate from piece-level scan-in.** Matches how Kojo and Procore log proof-of-delivery. *(Kojo, Procore)*
13. **Mirror the manufacturer's own "N of M" piece language in-app**, and model the data as (mark_id, piece_index, piece_count) — the same shape as GS1's ITIP standard — instead of inventing new vocabulary crews have to re-learn. *(Viwinco labels, GS1 ITIP)*
14. **Simple static yard-map graphic with a highlighted conex/bin, not a full 2D CAD floor plan.** Gets the "visual, not textual" win at a fraction of the effort of a true digital twin. *(SKUSavvy, CyberStockroom — scaled down)*
15. **Ask manufacturers' dispatch teams whether a scan-verified shipping manifest already exists** (several run FeneVision, which auto-generates one from production scans) before building receiving-reconciliation logic that assumes no expected list exists at all. *(FeneTech FeneVision)*

**Explicitly out of scope / not worth pursuing:** BLE proximity tags (Milwaukee ONE-KEY, DEWALT Tool Connect) — wrong tech for disposable, per-piece, yard-scale tracking; RFID (GED WinTrax) — real but premature at this volume/cost; dedicated voice-picking headsets — wrong form factor for a mobile, outdoor, phone-first crew; enterprise yard-management systems (Yard Spot, C3 Yard, RTLS) — built for scales two to three orders of magnitude larger than 8–15 conexes; full ERP suites (Cin7 Core, Katana, Fishbowl) — same feature shape as the simpler tools at 5–10x the price and complexity.
